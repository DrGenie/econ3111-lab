Week 1 · Episode 1 · 3 min 38 s

Introduction to Behavioural Economics and Rational Choice

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 1. The topics are the origins and methods of behavioural economics, and rational choice under certainty.

Narrator B: Let us start with an important distinction. A descriptive theory tells us how people actually make decisions. A normative theory tells us how people should make decisions. The theory of rational choice can be read in both ways. Behavioural economists usually argue that it is a useful normative benchmark but an incomplete descriptive theory.

Narrator A: Behavioural economics draws on several kinds of evidence. These include hypothetical choices, laboratory experiments with real incentives, field experiments, natural experiments, and process data such as eye tracking. Each method has strengths and weaknesses. Randomised experiments are especially valuable because random assignment allows us to identify causal effects.

Narrator B: The core of the week is the theory of choice under certainty. In this setting, every option leads to a known outcome. A person's preferences are described by a weak preference relation. We write x is weakly preferred to y when the person regards x as at least as good as y.

Narrator A: Two properties, or axioms, define rational preferences. Completeness requires that for any two options, the person either weakly prefers the first, or weakly prefers the second, or both. Transitivity requires that if x is at least as good as y, and y is at least as good as z, then x is at least as good as z.

Narrator B: Why does transitivity matter? Suppose a person strictly prefers tea to coffee, coffee to juice, and juice to tea. These preferences are cyclical. A trader could offer the person a sequence of swaps, each for a small fee, and the person would accept every swap, end up with the original drink, and be poorer. This argument is often called the money pump. It shows why cyclical preferences can be costly.

Narrator A: From weak preference we define indifference and strict preference. A person is indifferent between x and y if each is at least as good as the other. A person strictly prefers x to y if x is at least as good as y, but y is not at least as good as x.

Narrator B: A utility function represents preferences if it assigns a higher number to every option that is preferred. Utility in this sense is ordinal. Only the ranking matters. If the utility of x is ten and the utility of y is five, we can say that x is preferred to y, but not that x is twice as good.

Narrator A: Rational choice means choosing the most preferred option from the set of options that are available, which is called the menu or the feasible set. In consumer theory the feasible set is the budget set. The optimal bundle is usually found where an indifference curve is tangent to the budget line.

Narrator B: Finally, remember that rationality in this technical sense does not mean selfishness. A rational person may care about other people. Rationality only requires that preferences are consistent.

Narrator A: In the online laboratory for this week, you can test whether your own preferences between four items are complete and transitive. Next week, we study why the context of a decision can change what people choose.

Week 2 · Episode 2 · 3 min 50 s

Choice under Certainty (continued)

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 2. This week continues our study of decisions under certainty and introduces several important departures from the rational benchmark.

Narrator B: The first concept is opportunity cost. The opportunity cost of an option is the value of the best alternative that you give up when you choose it. A rational decision maker chooses an option only if its value is at least as great as its opportunity cost. Evidence shows that people often neglect opportunity costs, especially when those costs are not presented as explicit payments.

Narrator A: The second concept is sunk cost. A sunk cost is a cost that has already been incurred and cannot be recovered. Because a sunk cost is the same whatever you choose next, it should not affect the decision. People who continue an activity because they have already paid for it commit the sunk cost fallacy. When organisations continue to invest in failing projects for this reason, this is called escalation of commitment.

Narrator B: The third group of concepts concerns how the context of a choice affects the choice. Anchoring and adjustment describes the tendency to start from an initial number, even an irrelevant one, and to adjust insufficiently away from it. Research by Ariely, Loewenstein and Prelec found that people's valuations of ordinary goods were related to the last two digits of their social security numbers. They called this pattern coherent arbitrariness: absolute valuations were arbitrary, but relative valuations were consistent.

Narrator A: The decoy effect, also called the attraction effect, occurs when adding an option that is clearly worse than one of the existing options increases the share of people choosing that option. The compromise effect occurs when adding an extreme option makes a middle option more attractive. Both violate the principle that the relative ranking of two options should not depend on which other options are available.

Narrator B: Next is loss aversion. People evaluate outcomes as gains or losses relative to a reference point, and losses have a larger impact than gains of the same size. Loss aversion helps explain the endowment effect: people often demand more to give up a good than they would pay to acquire it.

Narrator A: Framing effects occur when different descriptions of the same options lead to different choices. For example, a programme described in terms of lives saved may be preferred to the same programme described in terms of lives lost.

Narrator B: Choice overload refers to the possibility that a larger choice set can reduce the likelihood of choosing at all, or lower satisfaction with the choice. The evidence on choice overload is mixed, and effects appear mainly when options are difficult to compare.

Narrator A: Finally, mental accounting describes how people group outcomes into separate mental accounts. The shape of the value function implies that people prefer to receive gains separately but to combine losses. These are known as the principles of segregating gains and integrating losses.

Narrator B: This week, Australian courts and regulators were relevant examples. The Federal Court found in May 2026 that some supermarket discount claims were misleading, and new laws on unfair trading practices begin in July 2027. In the online laboratory, you can design a decoy and test the mental accounting principles yourself.

Week 3 · Episode 3 · 3 min 32 s

Judgement under Risk and Uncertainty

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 3. The topic is probability theory, which is the benchmark for judging how well people reason about uncertain events.

Narrator B: A probability is a number between zero and one assigned to an event. Several rules follow. If two events are mutually exclusive, the probability that one or the other occurs is the sum of their probabilities. The probability that an event does not occur is one minus the probability that it occurs. If two events are independent, the probability that both occur is the product of their probabilities.

Narrator A: It is important not to confuse mutually exclusive events with independent events. If two events cannot both happen, then learning that one has happened tells you that the other has not. Such events are therefore not independent.

Narrator B: The conditional probability of A given B is the probability that A occurs, given that B has occurred. It equals the probability that both A and B occur, divided by the probability of B. The rule of total probability allows us to calculate the probability of an event by adding up its probability in each of several mutually exclusive cases, weighted by the probability of each case.

Narrator A: Bayes' rule allows us to reverse a conditional probability. It tells us the probability of a hypothesis given the evidence, using the probability of the evidence given the hypothesis and the prior probability of the hypothesis.

Narrator B: Consider an example. Suppose five per cent of essays in a course are written with artificial intelligence. A detection tool identifies ninety-eight per cent of such essays, but also wrongly flags four per cent of essays written by students. If an essay is flagged, what is the probability that it was written with artificial intelligence?

Narrator A: The easiest method is to imagine one thousand essays. Fifty are written with artificial intelligence, and forty-nine of these are flagged. Nine hundred and fifty are written by students, and thirty-eight of these are also flagged. So eighty-seven essays are flagged, and forty-nine of them were written with artificial intelligence. The probability is forty-nine divided by eighty-seven, which is about fifty-six per cent.

Narrator B: Many people expect a much higher answer, because they confuse the probability of a flag given artificial intelligence with the probability of artificial intelligence given a flag. The difference is driven by the base rate. In October 2025 the ABC reported that an Australian university had relied on a detection tool in many misconduct cases, and later stopped using it.

Narrator A: Bayesian updating is the process of revising beliefs as new evidence arrives. Each piece of evidence multiplies the prior odds by the likelihood ratio. When people update in this way, different initial beliefs tend to converge as evidence accumulates.

Narrator B: In the online laboratory, you can change the base rate in this example and see how the answer changes. You can also play the Monty Hall problem many times and observe why switching wins two times out of three.

Week 4 · Episode 4 · 3 min 9 s

Judgement under Risk and Uncertainty (continued)

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 4. This week we study how people's probability judgements depart from the rules of probability.

Narrator B: Kahneman and Tversky argued that people often rely on heuristics, which are simple rules of thumb. Heuristics are usually useful, but they can lead to systematic errors, which are called biases.

Narrator A: The representativeness heuristic is the tendency to judge the probability that something belongs to a category by how similar it is to a typical member of that category. In the well-known Linda problem, many people judge that Linda is more likely to be a bank teller who is active in the feminist movement than a bank teller. This is the conjunction fallacy. A conjunction of two events can never be more probable than either event alone.

Narrator B: The disjunction fallacy is the opposite mistake: underestimating the probability that at least one of several events occurs. If a project has ten stages, and each stage succeeds with probability zero point nine, the probability that all stages succeed is only about thirty-five per cent. This helps explain the planning fallacy, the tendency to underestimate how long projects will take and how much they will cost. The Sydney Opera House, estimated at seven million dollars, eventually cost one hundred and two million dollars.

Narrator A: The gambler's fallacy is the belief that, after a run of one outcome, the opposite outcome is more likely, even though the events are independent. It is related to the law of small numbers, the tendency to expect small samples to resemble the population. Interestingly, recent research by Miller and Sanjurjo found that the original studies of the so-called hot hand in basketball contained a statistical bias, and that a modest hot hand effect may exist.

Narrator B: Base-rate neglect is the tendency to ignore the prior probability when evaluating evidence. In the textbook example of mammogram screening, only about eight per cent of women who test positive actually have breast cancer, because the condition is rare in the population being screened.

Narrator A: Confirmation bias is the tendency to search for and interpret evidence in ways that support existing beliefs. Two people who receive the same ambiguous evidence may both become more confident in their original, opposite views.

Narrator B: The availability heuristic is the tendency to judge the frequency of events by how easily examples come to mind. Vivid and recent events, such as the shark attacks in Sydney in January 2026, may be judged to be more common than they are, while more frequent but less reported risks, such as drowning, receive less attention.

Narrator A: In the online laboratory, you can test whether you can produce a sequence that looks random, and you can classify a set of short cases by the heuristic involved.

Week 5 · Episode 5 · 3 min 2 s

Choice under Risk and Uncertainty

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 5. This week we study the standard theory of choice when outcomes are not known with certainty.

Narrator B: The textbook distinguishes between choice under risk, where the probabilities of outcomes are known, and choice under uncertainty, where the probabilities are unknown. A decision problem can be written as a matrix, with acts as rows, states of the world as columns, and outcomes in the cells.

Narrator A: Under uncertainty, several decision rules are available. The maximin rule chooses the act with the best worst outcome. The maximax rule chooses the act with the best best outcome. The minimax regret rule first calculates, for each state, the regret of each act, which is the difference between the best outcome available in that state and the outcome of the act. It then chooses the act whose largest regret is smallest.

Narrator B: Under risk, the simplest rule is to maximise expected value, which is the probability-weighted average of the outcomes. However, expected value cannot explain why people buy insurance, or why few people would pay much to play the St Petersburg game, which has an infinite expected value.

Narrator A: Daniel Bernoulli's solution was expected utility. Instead of averaging money, we average the utility of money. If the utility function is concave, so that each extra dollar adds less utility than the one before, then a person will be risk averse and will prefer a sure amount to a gamble with the same expected value.

Narrator B: The certainty equivalent of a gamble is the sure amount of money that gives the same utility as the gamble. For a risk-averse person, the certainty equivalent is less than the expected value. The difference is called the risk premium. It is the amount a person would be willing to give up to avoid the risk, and it explains why insurance can benefit both the buyer and the insurer.

Narrator A: A person with a linear utility function is risk neutral, and a person with a convex utility function is risk seeking. In the textbook example, a tablet costing three hundred and twenty-five dollars comes with an optional warranty of seventy-nine dollars. In expected value terms, the warranty is worthwhile only if the probability of the tablet breaking is about twenty-four per cent or more.

Narrator B: This week's current issue concerns home insurance. Premiums in Australia have risen strongly as climate risks have increased, and some households now under-insure or do not insure. Expected utility theory helps us calculate the highest premium a household should be willing to pay.

Narrator A: In the online laboratory, you can measure your own attitude to risk with the Holt and Laury task, calculate certainty equivalents, and play a version of Deal or No Deal.

Week 6 · Episode 6 · 3 min 16 s

Choice under Risk and Uncertainty (continued)

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 6. This week we examine evidence that people's choices under risk violate expected utility theory, and we study prospect theory.

Narrator B: Expected utility theory implies the sure-thing principle. If two options give the same outcome in some state of the world, that state should not affect which option is preferred.

Narrator A: The Allais problem shows a common violation. In the first decision, most people prefer one million dollars for certain to a gamble with a ten per cent chance of five million, an eighty-nine per cent chance of one million, and a one per cent chance of nothing. In the second decision, most people prefer a ten per cent chance of five million to an eleven per cent chance of one million. These two choices cannot both be explained by any expected utility function. The pattern is called the certainty effect.

Narrator B: The Ellsberg problem shows a different violation. An urn contains thirty red balls and sixty black or yellow balls in an unknown proportion. Most people prefer to bet on red rather than black, but prefer to bet on black or yellow rather than red or yellow. No single probability for black can explain both choices. People seem to prefer known probabilities to unknown probabilities. This is called ambiguity aversion.

Narrator A: One way to model ambiguity aversion is to assume that a person considers a set of possible probability distributions, called multiple priors, and evaluates each option by its worst case.

Narrator B: Prospect theory, developed by Kahneman and Tversky, has two main components. The first is a value function defined over gains and losses relative to a reference point. It is concave for gains, convex for losses, and steeper for losses than for gains, which captures loss aversion. The second is a probability weighting function, which overweights small probabilities and underweights moderate and large probabilities.

Narrator A: Together, these components produce what is called the fourfold pattern of risk attitudes. People tend to be risk seeking for small-probability gains, which explains lottery tickets, and risk averse for small-probability losses, which explains insurance. For large probabilities, the pattern is reversed.

Narrator B: The current issue this week is gambling advertising. In April 2026, the Australian Government announced reforms, expected to start in 2027, that would ban gambling advertisements during live sport broadcasts in the daytime and restrict odds-based advertising. Probability weighting helps explain why advertisements that emphasise large potential prizes can be effective.

Narrator A: In the online laboratory, you can adjust the value and weighting functions and see how they change the evaluation of a gamble, and you can check whether your own choices in the Allais and Ellsberg problems are consistent with expected utility.

Week 7 · Episode 7 · 2 min 53 s

Intertemporal Choice

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 7. Intertemporal choice refers to decisions in which costs and benefits occur at different points in time.

Narrator B: We begin with interest. Simple interest is paid only on the original amount. Compound interest is paid on the original amount and on interest already earned. Over long periods, compounding makes a large difference. One thousand dollars borrowed at twenty per cent per year grows to almost two thousand five hundred dollars after five years if no payments are made.

Narrator A: The present value of an amount received in the future is the amount that would grow to that sum if invested today at the market interest rate. If you can borrow and lend at the same interest rate, you should choose the option with the highest present value.

Narrator B: The exponential discounted utility model, or EDU model, describes how a person values a stream of utilities over time. Utility received t periods from now is multiplied by delta to the power t, where delta is the discount factor. A smaller delta means that the person is more impatient. The discount rate rho is related to delta by the formula delta equals one divided by one plus rho.

Narrator A: A key property of the EDU model is time consistency. Whether a person prefers a smaller reward earlier or a larger reward later depends only on the length of the delay between them, not on when the choice is made. Plans made today will still be followed tomorrow.

Narrator B: However, the evidence shows several departures from the EDU model. People often display very high discount rates over short delays. In a study by Thaler, the implied annual discount rate fell from about two hundred and seventy-seven per cent for a delay of three months to about sixty-three per cent for a delay of three years. People also discount small amounts more than large amounts, and gains more than losses. Most importantly, preferences may reverse as a reward comes closer.

Narrator A: This week's current issues concern credit. Since June 2025, buy now pay later providers in Australia have required a credit licence. Also in 2025, legislation reduced student loan debts by twenty per cent. Present value calculations help us evaluate such products.

Narrator B: In the online laboratory, you can answer a series of choices between money now and money later, and the laboratory will estimate your personal discount factor and check whether your choices are time consistent.

Week 8 · Episode 8 · 3 min 4 s

Intertemporal Choice (continued)

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 8. This week we study hyperbolic discounting, commitment, misprediction and miswanting.

Narrator B: The beta-delta model, also called quasi-hyperbolic discounting, adds a second parameter, beta, to the exponential model. All future utilities are multiplied by beta, in addition to the usual discounting by delta. When beta is less than one, the person gives special weight to the present. This is called present bias.

Narrator A: Present bias produces time inconsistency. In the textbook example, a person on Friday plans to skip a slice of cake at a party on Saturday, because the benefit of feeling well on Sunday outweighs the pleasure of the cake. But on Saturday, the cake is in the present, and the same person chooses to eat it.

Narrator B: The textbook distinguishes between impatience, which is captured by a low delta, and impulsivity, which is captured by a low beta. An impatient person discounts the future heavily but consistently. An impulsive person changes their mind as the moment of choice approaches.

Narrator A: People who are present biased may be naive or sophisticated. A naive person believes that their future self will follow their current plans. A sophisticated person understands that their future self will also be present biased. A naive person tends to procrastinate. A sophisticated person may act earlier, and may choose to restrict their own future choices. Such restrictions are called commitment devices. Examples include layaway plans, savings accounts that cannot be accessed early, and limits on smartphone use.

Narrator B: Evidence supports these ideas. DellaVigna and Malmendier found that gym members on monthly contracts paid more per visit than they would have paid with a pay-per-visit pass, because they overestimated how often they would attend.

Narrator A: The final topic is misprediction and miswanting. People often mispredict how much they will enjoy future experiences, and they may want things that do not increase their well-being. According to the peak-end rule, people remember an experience mainly by its most intense moment and its end, and they largely neglect its duration.

Narrator B: The current issue this week is Australia's minimum age for social media accounts, which began on the tenth of December 2025. By January 2026, about four point seven million accounts had been deactivated or restricted, but a survey by the eSafety Commissioner found that most children under sixteen were still using restricted platforms several months later.

Narrator A: In the online laboratory, you can compare how exponential, naive and sophisticated decision makers behave in the textbook's movie example, and test the peak-end rule.

Week 9 · Episode 9 · 2 min 51 s

Strategic Interaction

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 9. Until now, outcomes depended only on the decision maker and on the state of the world. In strategic interaction, outcomes also depend on the choices of other people.

Narrator B: A game consists of players, the strategies available to each player, and the payoffs that result from each combination of strategies. A best response is a strategy that gives a player the highest payoff, given the strategies of the other players.

Narrator A: A Nash equilibrium is a combination of strategies in which every player is playing a best response to the strategies of the others. In a Nash equilibrium, no player can gain by changing strategy alone.

Narrator B: Several games illustrate the concept. In a pure coordination game, such as two friends choosing a coffee shop, there are two equilibria and both players want to coordinate. In the battle of the sexes, players want to coordinate but disagree about where. In the prisoners' dilemma, each player has a dominant strategy to defect, and the unique equilibrium is worse for both players than mutual cooperation. The equilibrium is therefore not Pareto efficient.

Narrator A: Some games have no equilibrium in pure strategies, for example rock, paper, scissors. In such games, players may use mixed strategies, choosing each pure strategy with some probability. In a mixed strategy equilibrium, each player mixes in a way that makes the other player indifferent between their strategies.

Narrator B: Sequential games are solved by backward induction. We begin with the last move and work backwards. The resulting equilibrium is called subgame perfect, and it rules out threats that a player would not carry out if the time came. Such threats are called non-credible.

Narrator A: Experiments show that people do not always behave as these models predict. In the centipede game, for example, backward induction predicts that the first player ends the game immediately, but in practice most players continue for several rounds and both earn more.

Narrator B: The current issue this week is the use of game theory to analyse tariff disputes. In 2025, the NPR program Planet Money discussed whether the trade conflicts between the United States and its trading partners were best described as a prisoners' dilemma, a game of chicken, or a repeated game in which reputation matters.

Narrator A: In the online laboratory, you can enter any two-by-two game and find all of its equilibria, and you can play a repeated prisoners' dilemma against computer strategies such as tit for tat.

Week 10 · Episode 10 · 3 min 0 s

Strategic Interaction (continued)

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 10. This week we study behavioural game theory: social preferences, intentions and reciprocity, and limited strategic thinking.

Narrator B: In the ultimatum game, a proposer divides a sum of money and a responder either accepts or rejects. If the responder rejects, both players receive nothing. If players care only about their own money, the responder should accept any positive amount, and the proposer should offer the smallest possible amount. In experiments, however, proposers typically offer forty to fifty per cent, and offers below about twenty per cent are often rejected.

Narrator A: The textbook stresses that these results do not refute game theory. Game theory is defined in terms of utility, not money. If people care about the payoffs of others, rejecting an unfair offer may be a best response. Angner describes several distributional preferences, including altruism, utilitarian preferences, envy, Rawlsian or maximin preferences, and inequality aversion.

Narrator B: In the dictator game, the responder cannot reject. Many proposers still give money, which suggests genuine concern for others. However, giving falls when people can avoid the situation unobserved. This suggests that some giving reflects face-saving concerns, that is, a desire to be seen as fair.

Narrator A: Intentions also matter. A proposer who offers two dollars when the only alternative was to offer nothing may be seen as kind, while the same offer is seen as unkind when an equal split was possible. People reward kind intentions, which is positive reciprocity, and punish unkind intentions, which is negative reciprocity. Reciprocity helps explain trust in the trust game and cooperation in public goods games, especially when free riders can be punished.

Narrator B: Finally, people often do not reason many steps ahead. In the beauty contest game, each player chooses a number from zero to one hundred, and the winner is the person closest to two thirds of the average. The Nash equilibrium is zero, but most people choose numbers between twenty and thirty-five. Level-k models describe players who reason a limited number of steps.

Narrator A: The current issue this week concerns fairness in pricing. In September 2025, the United Kingdom competition regulator reported on ticket sales for the Oasis reunion concerts. The case illustrates why buyers may react strongly to prices that they regard as unfair.

Narrator B: In the online laboratory, you can make offers to computer players with different social preferences, simulate the beauty contest, and observe contributions in a public goods game with and without punishment.

Week 11 · Episode 11 · 3 min 3 s

Choice Architecture

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 11. The topic is choice architecture and behavioural public policy.

Narrator B: Choice architecture refers to the design of the context in which people make decisions, including the order of options, the default option, and the way information is presented. There is no neutral way to present a choice, so someone always acts as a choice architect.

Narrator A: Libertarian paternalism is the view that choice architects may steer people towards options that make them better off, as judged by themselves, while preserving their freedom to choose. A nudge is any aspect of choice architecture that changes behaviour in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing economic incentives.

Narrator B: It is important to distinguish nudges from other policy tools. A ban removes options. A tax or subsidy changes incentives. A nudge does neither. For example, placing fruit at eye level in a cafeteria is a nudge, while a ban on large sugary drinks is not.

Narrator A: Defaults are among the most effective nudges. Johnson and Goldstein found much higher rates of consent to organ donation in countries with presumed consent than in countries where people must opt in. Madrian and Shea found that automatic enrolment raised participation in a retirement savings plan from about thirty-seven to eighty-six per cent among new employees.

Narrator B: The Save More Tomorrow programme of Thaler and Benartzi asked workers to commit in advance to save part of their future pay rises. It uses present bias, loss aversion and inertia, and it raised average savings rates substantially over time.

Narrator A: The opposite of a helpful nudge is sludge: unnecessary frictions that make it harder to act in one's own interest, such as complicated cancellation procedures. From July 2027, Australian law will prohibit unfair trading practices, including some forms of subscription traps and hidden fees.

Narrator B: The textbook discusses several criticisms of the nudge agenda. Choice architects may themselves be biased. Paternalism may be objectionable even when choices are preserved. There may be a slippery slope towards more coercive policies. And nudges may be too weak to solve large problems. Recent research by DellaVigna and Linos found that the average effect of nudges tested by government units was about one point four percentage points, much smaller than the average effect reported in academic journals.

Narrator A: In the online laboratory, you can design a choice environment for a simulated population of students and classify policies as nudges, incentives or bans.

Week 12 · Episode 12 · 2 min 43 s

Assessing Behavioural Economics

Transcript

Narrator A: This is the ECON3111 podcast for Week 12, the final episode. This week we assess the achievements and limitations of behavioural economics.

Narrator B: Angner concludes that neoclassical economics is often criticised unfairly. It does not assume that people are selfish, that they seek only pleasure, or that they perform calculations in their heads. Many such assumptions are auxiliary assumptions that can be replaced.

Narrator A: At the same time, the evidence shows that people deviate from the standard theory in systematic ways. They honour sunk costs, rely on heuristics, violate the sure-thing principle, display present bias, and reason only a few steps ahead in strategic situations. Because these deviations are systematic, they can be described by scientific models.

Narrator B: Behavioural economics must also be assessed critically. In 2015, a large project attempted to replicate one hundred studies in psychology, and only about thirty-six per cent produced significant results again. In experimental economics, eleven of eighteen studies replicated. Some well-known findings did not replicate, while others, including anchoring, default effects and the main patterns of prospect theory, have been confirmed in large studies.

Narrator A: Publication bias is an important problem. Journals are more likely to publish significant results, so published effects may overstate true effects. After correcting for publication bias, estimates of the average effect of nudges become much smaller.

Narrator B: Research integrity is also important. In May 2025, Harvard University revoked the tenure of a well-known researcher on honesty after a finding of research misconduct, which she has denied. A related study had been retracted in 2021. Such cases show why open data and replication are essential.
