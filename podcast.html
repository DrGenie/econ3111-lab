Week 0 · Episode 0

How to win this course

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

MESFIN: Welcome to Behave Yourself, the podcast for ECON3111. I'm Mesfin.

SAM: And I'm Sam. I took this course last year, so I'm here to ask the questions you are too polite to ask.

MESFIN: Go on then.

SAM: What is behavioural economics, in one sentence, without the word "irrational"?

MESFIN: It is economics that takes real people seriously. We start with a clean model of how a sensible person would choose, we test it against what people actually do, and where the model misses in a predictable way, we fix it.

SAM: Predictable is the key word, right?

MESFIN: It is. If people made random mistakes, there would be nothing to study. The interesting part is that the mistakes line up. People keep watching movies they hate because they paid for the ticket. They pay more for a box of chocolates if they have just written down a high number. They sign up for a gym and don't go. Those patterns are so regular that firms build business models on them.

SAM: Which is a bit creepy.

MESFIN: It can be. That's why this year we spend time on the law as well. From July 2027, Australia bans a set of tricks, like fees that appear at the last step of checkout, and subscriptions that are easy to start and hard to cancel. That law is behavioural economics written into legislation.

SAM: OK, practical stuff. How do people do well?

MESFIN: Three things. First, every week ask the same three questions: what does the benchmark predict, what pushes real people away from it, and what follows for a firm, a government, or you. If your exam answers follow that shape, you're most of the way there.

SAM: Second?

MESFIN: Build your one-page exam sheet as you go. Every week's slides end with a box called "Add to your A4 page". Do it that week, not in week twelve.

SAM: I did mine in week twelve. Do not recommend.

MESFIN: Third, play. Every week there's a lab on the course website: a money pump, a Bayes machine, a prospect theory studio. Ten minutes with a lab teaches you more than an hour re-reading slides.

SAM: And the League?

MESFIN: The Bias Hunters League. Teams of four, named after a bias. Points for games, exit tickets and examples you spot in the wild. It doesn't count towards your grade. It's there because a bit of competition makes the semester more fun, and yes, I know that's a nudge.

SAM: Last question. In week zero you made everyone write down a number between zero and a hundred. Why?

MESFIN: You'll find out in week ten. Keep the number somewhere.

SAM: That's the cliffhanger. See you in week one.

Week 1 · Episode 1

The money pump

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: Mesfin, this week's lecture opened with you trying to take people's money with bubble tea.

MESFIN: Legally. If you told me you prefer brown sugar to taro, taro to matcha, and matcha to brown sugar, I could sell you swaps for a dollar each, forever. You'd go round in a circle, getting poorer.

SAM: And that's why economists want transitivity.

MESFIN: Exactly. Rational preferences need two things. Completeness: you can compare any two options, even if your answer is "they're equally good". And transitivity: if A is at least as good as B, and B at least as good as C, then A is at least as good as C. That's the whole definition of rational in this course.

SAM: So rational doesn't mean smart.

MESFIN: And it doesn't mean selfish. You can be a rational philanthropist. It only means your wants fit together.

SAM: Then utility. Everyone thinks it means happiness.

MESFIN: It's a scoreboard. A utility function gives bigger numbers to things you prefer. That's it. If utility of A is 10 and B is 5, you prefer A. You can't say A is twice as good. Double every number and you represent exactly the same preferences.

SAM: Ordinal.

MESFIN: Ordinal. Then we put that inside a budget. Prices and income define what you can afford; you pick the best thing you can afford. With two goods that's the tangency picture you've seen since first year.

SAM: Where does behavioural economics come in?

MESFIN: Remember the thousand teacups? Each cup has one more grain of sugar than the last. You can't taste one grain, so you're indifferent between neighbours. Chain those indifferences and transitivity says cup one and cup one thousand are the same. But you can taste the difference. Small, unnoticed steps add up. That's how slow price creep and shrinkflation work.

SAM: And the AI bit was new this year.

MESFIN: A 2023 paper in PNAS gave a language model budget choices and checked them against the consistency rules. The model was more consistent than most humans. So here's a question to think about: if AI agents start shopping for us, does the rational benchmark become more realistic? And whose preferences is the agent maximising?

SAM: Homework for the bus. Try the money pump lab and see if you're pumpable.

Week 2 · Episode 2

The $20 that cost $30

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: This week you auctioned a twenty-dollar note and it sold for more than twenty dollars.

MESFIN: It usually does. The rule is that the second-highest bidder also pays. Once two people are at nineteen and eighteen, each thinks "one more dollar and I win twenty", and soon both are committed to losing money.

SAM: Because what they've already bid is gone.

MESFIN: It's sunk. The benchmark says ignore sunk costs; only future costs and benefits matter. But people find that very hard. Angner's example is a basketball ticket you paid eighty dollars for. A snowstorm hits. Many people would drive if they paid, but stay home if the ticket was free. The eighty dollars is gone either way.

SAM: And you brought in Snowy 2.0.

MESFIN: Announced in 2017 at two billion dollars, now a target of 22.2 billion with completion pushed to late 2029. People say it's too late to turn back. That might be correct, but only if the future benefits exceed the future costs. "We've already spent so much" is not an argument.

SAM: Then anchoring. The student-number thing.

MESFIN: Write the last two digits of your student number, then say what you'd pay for chocolates. People with higher digits tend to state higher prices. The number is meaningless, but it becomes a starting point and people adjust too little.

SAM: Which is what the Coles case was about.

MESFIN: In May 2026 the Federal Court found that Coles made misleading "Down Down" claims. The ACCC's case was that prices went up for a short time and then came "down" to a price at or above where they started. That only works on shoppers because the "was" price acts as an anchor.

SAM: Decoys next.

MESFIN: The Economist offered web for 59 dollars, print for 125, and print plus web for 125. Nobody picks print alone. But with it on the menu, most people chose print plus web. Without it, most chose web only. An option nobody picks changed what people picked.

SAM: And loss aversion, mental accounting.

MESFIN: Losses feel roughly twice as big as gains of the same size. That explains the endowment effect: owners ask about twice what buyers will pay for the same mug. And it gives Thaler's rules: separate gains, combine losses. Wrap presents separately; pay one bill, not five.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: Before any decision, cross out what's already spent, and decide on your own price before you see theirs.

Week 3 · Episode 3

Flagged

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: Scariest cold open of the semester.

MESFIN: An AI detector flags your essay. It catches 98 percent of AI essays and wrongly flags 4 percent of human ones. Five percent of essays in the course are AI-written. What's the chance yours was?

SAM: Most people said 98 or 94 percent.

MESFIN: And the answer is about 56. Picture a thousand essays. Fifty are AI, and 49 get flagged. Nine hundred and fifty are honest, and four percent of those, 38, get flagged anyway. So 87 flags, of which 49 are real. Fifty-six percent.

SAM: So almost half the flagged students did nothing wrong.

MESFIN: In that example, yes. The detector's accuracy is the probability of a flag given AI. The panel needs the probability of AI given a flag. Those are different numbers, and the gap between them is the base rate.

SAM: And this actually happened.

MESFIN: The ABC reported in October 2025 that one Australian university logged nearly 6,000 misconduct cases in 2024, about 90 percent about AI. The detector's maker warns its scores shouldn't be the only evidence. The university stopped using it.

SAM: OK, the rules. Give me the short version.

MESFIN: Probabilities are between zero and one. "Or" means add, if the events can't both happen. "Not" means one minus. "And" means multiply, if the events are independent. For "at least one", use one minus the chance of none.

SAM: And Monty Hall broke the room.

MESFIN: It always does. Pick one of three cups. The host, who knows where the coin is, lifts an empty one. Switching wins two thirds of the time. Your first pick is wrong two times in three, and when it's wrong, the host is forced to show you the other empty cup, so switching takes you straight to the coin.

SAM: And Bayes' rule itself.

MESFIN: Posterior is proportional to likelihood times prior. Or in odds form: posterior odds equal the likelihood ratio times prior odds. But honestly, in the exam, draw the thousand people. It's faster and you won't flip the conditional.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: Whenever someone tells you how accurate a test is, ask: how common is the thing it's testing for?

Week 4 · Episode 4

Sharks, streaks and Linda

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: You claimed you could spot fake coin flips from across the room.

MESFIN: And I mostly could. People who make up a sequence switch between heads and tails too often and avoid long runs. In thirty real flips there's about a 90 percent chance of at least one run of four. Fakes rarely have one.

SAM: That's representativeness.

MESFIN: Judging probability by resemblance. HHHHH doesn't look random, so we think tails is due. That's the gambler's fallacy. Each flip is independent; the coin has no memory.

SAM: But there was a twist about the hot hand.

MESFIN: For decades the hot hand in basketball was the classic example of seeing patterns in noise. Then in 2018 Miller and Sanjurjo showed the original analysis had a subtle bias. Corrected, there is a modest real hot hand. I like teaching that because it shows science correcting itself.

SAM: Linda.

MESFIN: Linda is a philosophy graduate who cared about social justice. Is she more likely a bank teller, or a bank teller who's active in the feminist movement? Most people pick the second. But every feminist bank teller is a bank teller. A conjunction can never be more likely than one of its parts.

SAM: And that links to Snowy again.

MESFIN: And to your assignment. A plan with ten steps, each 90 percent likely to go well, only has about a 35 percent chance of all ten going well. The Sydney Opera House was estimated at 7 million dollars for 1963 and opened in 1973 at 102 million. The fix is to look at how long similar projects took, not just your own plan.

SAM: Then the sharks.

MESFIN: In January 2026, Sydney had three shark attacks in about 26 hours, and beaches closed. It was frightening and it dominated the news. But Royal Life Saving records hundreds of drownings a year in Australia. Vivid, recent events feel more common than they are. That's availability.

SAM: And confirmation bias.

MESFIN: We look for evidence that supports what we already think. Ambiguous evidence can push two people further apart instead of together. Recommendation feeds make that worse.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: Ask for the base rate, convert to counts, multiply the chain, and look for the fact that would prove you wrong.

Week 5 · Episode 5

Deal or No Deal

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: Week five. Risk. You opened with five hundred dollars for sure or a coin flip for eleven hundred.

MESFIN: And most of the room took the five hundred, even though the gamble's expected value is 550. That isn't a mistake. It tells us that the value of money bends: the second five hundred dollars is worth less to you than the first.

SAM: Start with risk versus uncertainty.

MESFIN: Risk is when you can attach probabilities: a coin, insurance claims. Uncertainty is when you can't: will this start-up exist in 2030? Under uncertainty we looked at three rules. Maximin picks the best worst case. Maximax picks the best best case. Minimax regret picks the act whose worst regret is smallest.

SAM: And regret is computed column by column.

MESFIN: Please write that on your A4 page. It's the most common error on this topic.

SAM: Expected value next, with the tablet warranty.

MESFIN: A 325-dollar tablet, a 79-dollar warranty. It only pays off in expected value if the chance of breaking is about one in four. For most people it isn't, so the warranty has negative expected value. Yet people buy them. So do lottery tickets.

SAM: So we need expected utility.

MESFIN: Bernoulli's insight in 1738: the St Petersburg game has infinite expected value, but nobody pays much to play it. With a utility function that bends, like log or square root, expected utility is finite. With log utility the game's certainty equivalent is about four dollars.

SAM: And the certainty equivalent was the Deal or No Deal moment.

MESFIN: The lowest sure offer you'd accept instead of the gamble. If it's below the expected value, you're risk averse. The difference is the risk premium, and that premium is exactly why insurance can be a good deal for both you and the insurer.

SAM: Which got awkward with the climate example.

MESFIN: Home insurance premiums have risen sharply as flood and fire risk grows. In our example, a household should insure at a premium up to about 4,700 dollars, but not at 6,000. When premiums pass people's risk premium, they drop cover. Then a disaster hits uninsured households.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: Before buying any add-on cover, estimate the chance you'll claim. Then ask how much the risk itself is worth to you.

Week 6 · Episode 6

Why one percent feels like five

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: The Allais vote caught most of the room.

MESFIN: It catches most rooms. A million dollars for sure, or a gamble with a 1 percent chance of nothing. Most people take the sure million. Then in a second pair, with the certain part removed, most people take the riskier option. Write the options as lottery tickets and you see the two decisions differ only in a column where both options pay the same. The sure-thing principle says that column shouldn't matter.

SAM: And expected utility can't fit both choices.

MESFIN: Four lines of algebra and you get a contradiction. The behavioural label is the certainty effect: going from 99 to 100 percent feels far bigger than going from 10 to 11.

SAM: Then the bags.

MESFIN: Bag K had ten red and ten black, known. Bag U had twenty, unknown mix. Most people choose bag K when betting on red, and bag K again when betting on black. But if you think U has fewer reds, it must have more blacks. You can't believe both. People simply prefer known odds. That's ambiguity aversion, Ellsberg's paradox.

SAM: Then prospect theory. The Nobel stuff.

MESFIN: Two parts. A value function over gains and losses from a reference point, steeper for losses. And decision weights instead of probabilities: small probabilities get too much weight, large ones too little.

SAM: Which explains lottery tickets and insurance in the same wallet.

MESFIN: Small chance of a big gain: overweighted, so you buy the ticket. Small chance of a big loss: overweighted, so you buy insurance. With high probabilities, it flips. That's the fourfold pattern.

SAM: And gambling ads.

MESFIN: In April 2026 the government announced reforms expected from January 2027: no gambling ads during live sport from 6 am to 8:30 pm, a cap of three an hour on free-to-air in that window, no celebrities or athletes, and no odds-style ads. Prospect theory tells you why odds ads matter. "Fifty-one dollars for a one-dollar bet" makes a vivid gain salient and invites you to overweight a tiny probability.

SAM: Same-game multis?

MESFIN: Overweighting small probabilities plus the conjunction fallacy from week four. Each leg feels likely; all of them together are not.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: When a probability is small, your gut is probably exaggerating it. Write the number down.

Week 7 · Episode 7

What is a dollar tomorrow worth?

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: A hundred dollars now or how much in a month?

MESFIN: If you said 110, your monthly discount rate is 10 percent. Compounded, that's over 200 percent a year. Your bank pays you four or five. Many people's answers imply discount rates far above any interest rate they could earn.

SAM: Start with interest.

MESFIN: Simple interest is paid only on the principal. Compound interest is interest on interest. A thousand dollars on a credit card at 20 percent, nothing repaid: about 2,500 after five years, and about 38,000 after twenty.

SAM: And the small loans.

MESFIN: Small amount credit contracts in Australia can charge a 20 percent establishment fee plus 4 percent a month. Borrow 500 for a month and you pay 120 in fees. Annualised, that's over a thousand percent.

SAM: Present value.

MESFIN: Bring future money into today: divide by one plus r to the power t. If you can borrow and lend at r, pick the option with the highest present value.

SAM: Then utility over time.

MESFIN: The exponential discounting model. Today's value of a stream is utility now plus delta times utility next period plus delta squared times the one after. Delta is your patience. And this model is time consistent: whether you prefer waiting a day depends only on the length of the wait, not on when you're asked.

SAM: But people aren't like that.

MESFIN: Thaler's 1981 study is the classic. People wanted about 30 dollars in three months instead of 15 now, 60 in a year, and 100 in three years. The implied annual rates fall from about 277 percent to 63 percent. Impatient now, patient about the far future.

SAM: And the marshmallow test update.

MESFIN: A 2018 replication found the link between preschool waiting time and later achievement was much smaller once family background was controlled for. Patience depends a lot on circumstances: if you can't trust the second marshmallow will come, eating the first is sensible.

SAM: BNPL and HECS.

MESFIN: Since June 2025, buy now pay later needs a credit licence and responsible lending checks. And in 2025 Parliament cut student debts by 20 percent. HECS has indexation, not real interest, so whether to repay early is a present-value question. With indexation below your savings rate, the maths usually says don't rush.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: Convert every "interest free" offer into an annual rate. Then compare.

Week 8 · Episode 8

Dear future me

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: Chocolate now or two at the end of class? And the same choice, but next week.

MESFIN: Many people took the chocolate now, but chose to wait next week. Same two-hour gap. Exponential discounting says your answers should match. They don't, because "now" is special. That's present bias.

SAM: Beta and delta.

MESFIN: Delta is ordinary patience. Beta is a one-off discount on everything that isn't now. With beta below one, the person you'll be on Saturday disagrees with the person you are on Friday. On Friday you plan to skip the cake. At the party, you eat it.

SAM: And the screening example.

MESFIN: Benny, with a low beta, plans to get the screening, then skips it when the day comes. That's why health systems mail test kits to your house. They remove the moment where present bias wins.

SAM: The movie game was brutal.

MESFIN: Four films, you must skip one. The naive procrastinator keeps thinking "I'll skip next week's instead" and ends up missing the best film. The sophisticated person knows their future self will procrastinate and acts earlier.

SAM: And gym memberships.

MESFIN: DellaVigna and Malmendier found people on monthly contracts paid over 70 dollars a month and went about four times, more than 17 dollars a visit, when a ten-visit pass was 10 dollars a visit. People buy for the person they plan to be.

SAM: Commitment devices.

MESFIN: Ulysses tied himself to the mast. Layaway, app timers, automatic transfers on payday, telling your team your draft date. Paying to remove your own future options sounds strange, unless you know your future self.

SAM: Then the under-16 ban.

MESFIN: It started on 10 December 2025. By January, about 4.7 million accounts had been deactivated or restricted. But an eSafety survey around three months in found more than eight in ten under-16s still using a restricted platform, often because age checks were weak. Is it a commitment device? A ban? Teams argued both ways.

SAM: And the peak-end rule.

MESFIN: We remember experiences by their worst moment and their end. Adding a slightly less painful tail made people prefer a longer painful episode. So end your presentation next week on a high.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: Don't plan for the ideal you. Plan for the you who'll be tired on Thursday night.

Week 9 · Episode 9

Split or steal

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: Split or steal. What did you choose?

MESFIN: I'll never tell. The game is a version of the prisoners' dilemma. Stealing is better for you whatever the other person does, so both steal and both get nothing, even though both splitting gives each of them half.

SAM: Nash equilibrium.

MESFIN: A set of strategies where everyone is playing a best response to everyone else. Nobody gains by changing on their own. In the prisoners' dilemma the equilibrium is both defecting, and it's worse for both than cooperating. Individually rational, collectively bad.

SAM: But coordination games are different.

MESFIN: Meeting your study partner at the same café: two equilibria, you just need to match. Battle of the sexes: two equilibria, but you disagree about which. Chicken: whoever commits first wins. Stag hunt: the good outcome needs trust.

SAM: And mixed strategies.

MESFIN: Rock, paper, scissors has no pure equilibrium. Everyone plays each with probability one third. The trick to computing a mixed equilibrium: each player mixes to make the other player indifferent.

SAM: The tournament.

MESFIN: Axelrod ran computer tournaments in the 1980s. The winner was tit-for-tat: cooperate first, then copy your opponent. In our class version, talking before playing raised cooperation, even though promises weren't binding.

SAM: Sequential games and threats.

MESFIN: Solve from the end backwards. Some threats aren't credible: once the other side has acted, carrying out the threat might hurt you more. Deterrence only works if the threat is believable.

SAM: Tariffs.

MESFIN: NPR's Planet Money did two episodes in 2025 using game theory on the tariff fights. Is it a prisoners' dilemma, where both sides lose, or chicken, where someone backs down? Or a repeated game where reputation matters? Students built both versions.

SAM: And The Traitors.

MESFIN: The faithful need to coordinate their vote on the right person. That's a coordination problem with hidden information. Focal points matter a lot.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: When others are choosing too, ask what they think you'll do.

Week 10 · Episode 10

Would you take two dollars?

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: The week-zero number finally came back.

MESFIN: Guess two thirds of the average. If everyone picks randomly, the average is 50, so a step-one thinker guesses 33. A step-two thinker guesses 22. Keep going and you reach zero, the Nash answer. But zero doesn't usually win, because most people stop after one or two steps. Being one step ahead of the crowd is what pays. That's level-k thinking.

SAM: The ultimatum game.

MESFIN: Split ten dollars. If the responder rejects, both get nothing. A purely selfish responder accepts anything, so the proposer should offer one dollar. In real experiments, offers are usually 40 to 50 percent, and offers under about 20 percent are rejected around half the time.

SAM: So people are irrational?

MESFIN: Angner says careful. Game theory is about utility, not dollars. If you dislike unfair splits, rejecting two dollars can be a best response. The selfishness was an extra assumption. Drop it and the theory works again.

SAM: Six social preferences.

MESFIN: Egoism, altruism, utilitarian, envy, Rawlsian, and inequality aversion. Each changes the game. If two prisoners are utilitarian, the dilemma disappears. If they're Rawlsian, it becomes a stag hunt.

SAM: Intentions.

MESFIN: The same eight-two split gets rejected more often when the proposer could have offered five-five than when the only alternative was ten-zero. It's not just what you get, it's why.

SAM: Public goods.

MESFIN: Put points in a pot that doubles and is shared. Selfishly, you should contribute nothing. Without punishment, contributions decay. With the option to pay to punish free riders, contributions rise and stay high, even though punishing never pays the punisher.

SAM: And the Oasis tickets.

MESFIN: The UK competition regulator found no evidence Ticketmaster used dynamic pricing, but fans weren't told there were two price tiers for standing tickets, with cheaper ones sold first. Fans felt cheated. Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler found the same in 1986: 82 percent said raising snow-shovel prices after a blizzard was unfair.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: Before you predict what people will do, ask who's playing, and what they think of you.

Week 11 · Episode 11

Who chose your default?

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: Germany 12 percent organ donor consent, Austria over 99. Same culture.

MESFIN: In Johnson and Goldstein's 2003 data. The difference was the default: opt-in versus opt-out. Nobody was forced or paid.

SAM: But you said that's not the whole story for Australia.

MESFIN: Registered consent isn't the same as transplants. Families, hospitals and systems matter. Some Australian experts argue opt-out alone wouldn't fix our rates.

SAM: Nudges.

MESFIN: Any part of choice architecture that predictably changes behaviour without banning options or significantly changing incentives. Easy and cheap to avoid. A sugar tax is an incentive. A ban on large sodas is a ban. Fruit at eye level is a nudge.

SAM: The classics.

MESFIN: Automatic enrolment in retirement savings lifted participation at one US firm from about 37 to 86 percent. Save More Tomorrow had workers commit to save part of future pay rises; savings rates went from 3.5 to 13.6 percent. It uses present bias, loss aversion and inertia all at once.

SAM: And sludge.

MESFIN: The opposite: friction that stops you doing what's good for you. One-click sign-up, phone-only cancellation. From July 2027 Australia's unfair trading practices law requires subscriptions to be easy to cancel and fees to be shown up front.

SAM: And the criticisms.

MESFIN: Choice architects have biases too. Paternalism, even soft paternalism, can be objectionable. Slippery slopes. And the big one: effects can be small. DellaVigna and Linos looked at 126 trials by two US nudge units. The average effect was about 1.4 percentage points, much smaller than in published papers, mostly because of publication bias.

SAM: And happiness.

MESFIN: If choices are biased, maximising revealed preferences may not make people better off. Happiness economics offers another yardstick, with its own problems. That's an open debate.

SAM: Takeaway?

MESFIN: Audit your own defaults this week: super fund, phone plan, notifications. Change one on purpose.

Week 12 · Episode 12

What survives

Audio coming soon. Read the transcript below.

Transcript and show notes

SAM: Last episode. Is behavioural economics any good?

MESFIN: Mostly, yes, with an asterisk. In 2015 a big project tried to replicate 100 psychology studies and only about 36 percent gave significant results again. In experimental economics, 11 of 18 replicated. Things improved after that: pre-registration, open data, big multi-lab studies.

SAM: What held up?

MESFIN: Anchoring, default effects, and the core prospect theory pattern, which replicated across 19 countries in 2020. What didn't: power posing raising hormones, and ego depletion, the idea that willpower runs out like fuel.

SAM: And fraud.

MESFIN: In May 2025 Harvard revoked the tenure of a well-known honesty researcher after a misconduct finding; she denies it. A co-authored study on signing honesty pledges at the top of a form had already been retracted in 2021. It's painful for the field. It's also the system working, because people checked.

SAM: And nudges shrank.

MESFIN: A 2022 meta-analysis said nudges had a medium effect. A re-analysis correcting for publication bias found little evidence of an average effect. The sensible view: many nudges work, effects are often modest and vary by context. Test, don't assume.

SAM: Angner's verdict?

MESFIN: Neoclassical economics isn't as silly as its critics say. It doesn't assume selfishness or mental calculation. But deviations are real and systematic: sunk costs, heuristics, Allais, present bias, limited strategic thinking. Because they're systematic, they can be modelled. Irrational doesn't mean unpredictable.

SAM: And exam advice, quickly.

MESFIN: Benchmark, deviation, implication. Every long answer. Use symbols for the benchmark, an example for the deviation, and evaluate the implication. And bring the A4 page you built week by week.

SAM: Anything to keep from this course?

MESFIN: Three questions, forever. What's the benchmark? What could be pushing me away from it? And who designed this choice, and why?

SAM: That's Behave Yourself. Thanks for listening.
